Reading Combate
by machine

Combate was a Portuguese workers' newspaper printed between 1974 and 1978, built out of hand-drawn cartoons, photographs, facsimiles of handwritten notes and headlines set by hand. This project reads that paper computationally — the text with OCR and HTR, and the drawings as objects in their own right.

The drawings carry the argument

In most newspapers the illustration decorates the article. In Combate it frequently is the article: a two-panel cartoon does the work of an editorial, a photographed wall poster reports a strike, a page of somebody's handwriting is reproduced whole because it came from the factory floor.

Treating those regions as noise, which is what a text-first OCR pipeline does, throws away a large part of what the paper actually said. So the project catalogues them: every drawing, photograph, engraving, masthead and handwritten facsimile gets coordinates, a type and a description, tied to the issue and page it came from.

The scans make this harder than it sounds. They are grey, low-contrast and unevenly exposed; ink bleeds through from the reverse of the sheet; the layout follows no template from one page to the next. Off-the-shelf OCR on these pages produces error rates that make the output unusable without heavy preprocessing.

That difficulty is also the reason the corpus is useful. It is a hard, real test case for OCR and text recognition under bad conditions, and it is open.

Method

The pipeline runs in five stages. Stages 1 and 2 are done for the whole corpus; stages 3 to 5 are in progress, issue by issue.

  1. Acquisition

    The 51 issues were collected as PDFs — 487 pages in total, between 2.2 and 8.1 MB per issue. The originals are held at the Biblioteca Nacional de Portugal; the digital surrogates used here carry the Marxists Internet Archive source line.

  2. Rasterisation

    Each page is rendered to a bitmap at a fixed scale so that every coordinate in the catalogue is normalised to the page box, from 0 to 1. This site renders pages in the browser with the same convention, which is why a region catalogued once can be recut at any resolution later.

  3. Layout segmentation

    Pages are split into text zones and graphic zones. Combate has no stable grid, so template matching fails; segmentation relies on ink-density and connected-component analysis, corrected by hand. Every region on this site can be verified against the page it came from.

  4. Text recognition

    Printed columns go through OCR after deskewing, denoising and contrast normalisation. Handwritten facsimiles need HTR with a model fine-tuned on the corpus itself — generic models fail on them, and there are enough of these pages that ignoring them would distort the record.

  5. Cataloguing the graphics

    Each graphic region is described, typed and stored with its issue, page and box. That catalogue is what the illustrations section reads from.

Catalogue entry

One record per region. Coordinates are fractions of the page, measured from the top-left corner.

{
  "issue": 20,          // issue number, 1-51
  "page": 5,            // 1-based page within the issue PDF
  "box": [0.05, 0.50, 0.36, 0.205],   // x, y, width, height (0-1)
  "type": "illustration",             // cartoon | photograph | engraving |
                                      // handwriting | masthead | composed block
  "title": "Massarelos residents' bulletin",
  "note": "Reproduced masthead of a neighbourhood committee bulletin."
}

Regions you mark in the viewer stay in the browser for the session only. Export them as JSON and add them to the catalogue file in the repository to make them permanent.

Why the scans fight back

Low contrast and heavy grain across most issues; darkened patches where the original was folded or the platen was uneven; bleed-through from the reverse side.

Cartoon outlines thin out or break under thresholding, which breaks contour-based detection; captions inside drawings are read as body text unless the region is masked first.

About

The project

COMBAT — Computational OCR/ATR Methods for Combate: Comics, Images and Text — applies machine reading to a newspaper that resists it, and treats its graphic content as evidence rather than decoration.

The newspaper

Combate ran from June 1974, weeks after the Carnation Revolution, into 1978. It described itself as a paper of the workers themselves, and it looked like it: hand-lettered headings, drawings by contributors, reprinted leaflets and communiqués, and a layout that changed from page to page.
